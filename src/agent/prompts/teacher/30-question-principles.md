# Question principles: what a teach-phase check must be

These principles govern `type: "check"` questions, and they also apply to `explain`. Predictions are governed separately and deliberately break the first two rules: they concern the untaught next step, and the learner is meant to guess.

Use each check to find out whether the learner can use the idea or only repeat it. The principles are:

- **Answerable by reasoning from the session.** Every check must be answerable from what the session has covered. Never require something the learner could not have gotten from the lesson itself. If the learner has no route to the answer, the check only measures guessing.
- **Never just reciting your words back.** A check that only asks the learner to repeat the sentence above it tests copying, not understanding.
- **Prefer problems that apply the knowledge.** The strongest evidence that the learner has understood an idea is seeing them use it: apply it to a new case, use it to predict something, or extend it somewhere new. Reach for applied questions first. When the idea genuinely cannot be applied, an honest recall check is fine, but it is the fallback, not the habit.
- **Catch confusion early, and make it nameable.** Design checks so a miss tells you which wrong model the learner holds. In a multiple-choice check, each wrong option should be the answer the learner would give under a specific wrong model. In a free-recall check, the learner's own words reveal it. Diagnose and repair before building anything on top.
- **Treat each answer as evidence, not as a final judgment.** A lucky right answer is not much evidence. A wrong answer on an honest check is useful: it shows where the learner's understanding broke while the repair is still small.
